# Participation, Contribution, and Access

Decision Point Initiative is designed to be open to useful contribution without making any individual organizer's attention the scarce resource that participation depends on.

The governing principle is simple:

> **Open contribution does not imply open personal access.**

DPI should make it easy for people to contribute evidence, criticism, experiments, reproductions, work packets, and synthesis while keeping communication, authority, affiliation, and access proportional to demonstrated trust and responsibility.

## 1. The institution is the collaborative surface

The workshop, repository, issue templates, work packets, and other designated channels are the primary places to participate.

A contributor should not need a private relationship with the founder, an organizer, or a council member in order to do meaningful work. Likewise, useful participation does not create an expectation of private correspondence, direct messages, meetings, personal contact information, or continuing one-to-one collaboration.

DPI should increasingly route participation through inspectable institutional processes rather than informal personal access.

## 2. Initiative is welcome; scope is governed

DPI welcomes initiative, including criticism and work that challenges the Initiative's own assumptions. But initiative is most useful when it is attached to a clear decision point, claim, work packet, test, artifact, or falsifier.

When possible, contributors should answer:

- What decision point or work packet does this address?
- What claim is being made or challenged?
- What evidence supports it?
- What baseline or alternative should it be compared against?
- What test could distinguish the competing explanations?
- What result would falsify or materially weaken the claim?

Large unsolicited projects are not inherently more valuable than bounded contributions. Scope should be negotiated before substantial work is undertaken when review, collaboration, compensation, or institutional adoption is expected.

## 3. No retroactive obligation from unsolicited work

Absent an explicit agreement in advance, unsolicited research, analysis, designs, audits, prototypes, documents, or other work do not create an expectation of:

- review or detailed feedback;
- a response within any particular period;
- compensation or reimbursement;
- continued correspondence;
- a meeting or private access;
- appointment, partnership, employment, advisory status, or other affiliation;
- adoption, publication, endorsement, or future collaboration.

A person may choose to contribute substantial effort, but the amount of voluntarily invested effort does not by itself expand that person's claim on DPI's time, attention, money, authority, or relationships.

If a contributor wants DPI to commit resources before undertaking substantial work, that commitment should be discussed and agreed to first.

## 4. Contribution does not imply affiliation

Participation in a workshop does not make someone a representative, employee, partner, fellow, advisor, judge, council member, spokesperson, or agent of DPI.

Affiliation exists only when DPI explicitly grants or agrees to that role.

Contributors should describe their relationship to DPI accurately and should not imply endorsement, authority, or institutional status that has not been granted.

## 5. Access expands through demonstrated stewardship

DPI should distinguish between openness to ideas and access to scarce institutional resources.

Greater responsibility or access may develop through repeated evidence of qualities such as:

- useful completed work;
- accuracy and intellectual honesty;
- respect for scope;
- reliability over time;
- ability to disagree constructively;
- appropriate handling of uncertainty, provenance, and sensitive information;
- restraint with other people's time and attention;
- sound judgment about when escalation or direct contact is actually necessary.

Enthusiasm alone is not a substitute for those qualities.

This is not a judgment of a person's worth. It is governance of access to finite resources.

## 6. Founder and organizer attention should sit near synthesis, not intake

As participation grows, DPI should avoid architectures in which every contribution requires direct founder review.

The desired flow is:

```text
CONTRIBUTION
→ STRUCTURED INTAKE
→ WORK-PACKET / CLAIM LINKAGE
→ PEER REVIEW / REPRODUCTION / CHALLENGE
→ SYNTHESIS
→ ESCALATION WHEN DECISION-RELEVANT
→ ORGANIZER / COUNCIL ATTENTION
```

The goal is not to prevent organizers from seeing raw work. It is to ensure that the institution can grow without requiring one person to absorb every message, artifact, dispute, or idea.

Future tooling and governance should therefore favor summaries that surface:

- what materially changed;
- what evidence is new;
- which claim is affected;
- whether a result reproduced;
- whether a falsifier was triggered;
- what uncertainty remains;
- what decision, if any, now requires escalation.

## 7. Communication is not an unlimited support channel

Public participation channels are for advancing the work. They are not a guarantee of ongoing personal discussion, emotional processing, rapid response, or unlimited review.

Organizers may decline, defer, summarize, redirect, archive, or leave unanswered communications that are duplicative, out of scope, disproportionately demanding, or better handled through an existing work packet or public process.

Where a structured channel exists, contributors should use it rather than attempting to bypass the process through repeated private outreach.

## 8. Collaboration must be explicit

A contribution can be useful without becoming a collaboration.

When DPI wants a deeper working relationship with a contributor, the scope should become explicit: the problem, role, authority, expected deliverable, communication channel, review cadence, confidentiality if any, compensation if any, and conditions for ending or changing the arrangement.

This protects both sides from assumptions that were never mutually agreed.

## 9. Design objective

The desired culture can be summarized as:

> **Everyone may approach the work. Not everyone receives the same access to the people, authority, or resources around it.**

Or more compactly:

> **Initiative is welcomed. Scope is governed. Access is earned.**

This boundary is intended to make DPI more hospitable, not less. A strong institution gives people meaningful ways to contribute without requiring personal proximity to its founder or organizers, and it allows scarce human attention to remain focused on the decisions where it is most valuable.
